Overview

This homework covered geometric modeling from Bézier evaluation through triangle-mesh processing. I implemented de Casteljau subdivision for curves and tensor-product surfaces, then used the half-edge data structure to compute area-weighted normals and perform local edge flips and splits. Finally, I combined the local operations with Loop subdivision rules to refine entire meshes. The main lesson was that mesh algorithms require both correct geometry and careful maintenance of every connectivity pointer.

Section I: Bezier Curves and Surfaces

Part 1: Bezier curves with 1D de Casteljau subdivision

De Casteljau's algorithm repeatedly interpolates adjacent control points. Given points \(p_i\) and parameter \(t\), one level is computed with \(p'_i=(1-t)p_i+t p_{i+1}\). Each level has one fewer point, and the final single point lies on the Bézier curve. My evaluateStep implementation visits each adjacent pair once, computes this interpolation, and returns the next level. I created a custom degree-five curve with six control points and used the viewer to inspect every level.

Part 2: Bezier surfaces with separable 1D de Casteljau

I extended the interpolation step to 3D points. To evaluate a surface at \((u,v)\), I evaluate every row of the control-point grid at \(u\), producing one point per row. Those points form a second Bézier curve, which I evaluate at \(v\). This separable pair of 1D evaluations produces the final point on the tensor-product surface.

Section II: Triangle Meshes and Half-Edge Data Structure

Part 3: Area-weighted vertex normals

I traverse the one-ring neighborhood of a vertex with h = h->twin()->next(). For each incident non-boundary triangle, I take the cross product of two triangle edges. Its magnitude is twice the triangle area, so summing these unnormalized face normals automatically applies area weighting. Normalizing the sum gives the final unit vertex normal used for smooth shading.

Part 4: Edge flip

For an interior edge, I gathered the six local half-edges, four vertices, five edges, and two faces. I changed the shared diagonal to connect the two formerly opposite vertices, then explicitly reassigned every local half-edge's next, twin, vertex, edge, and face pointers. I also updated the representative half-edge stored by every adjacent element. The operation allocates or deletes nothing and ignores boundary edges. Labeling the local neighborhood before coding was the most useful debugging technique.

Part 5: Edge split

Splitting an interior edge inserts one midpoint vertex and changes two triangles into four. I reused the original local elements and allocated exactly one vertex, three edges, six half-edges, and two faces. I placed the new vertex at the average of the original endpoints and explicitly assigned every pointer in the new neighborhood. Boundary edges are ignored. For Loop subdivision, the two pieces along the original edge are marked old, while the new cross-triangle edges are marked new.

Part 6: Loop subdivision for mesh upsampling

I first compute all new positions without modifying the original mesh. An old vertex of degree \(n\) uses \((1-nu)p+u\sum_i p_i\), where \(u=3/16\) for degree three and \(u=3/(8n)\) otherwise. A new point on an edge with endpoints \(A,B\) and opposite vertices \(C,D\) uses \(\frac{3}{8}(A+B)+\frac{1}{8}(C+D)\). I save the original edge iterators, split each exactly once, flip only new edges connecting one old and one new vertex, and finally copy the stored positions into the mesh.

Each iteration quadruples the triangle count and rounds sharp corners. The original cube becomes slightly asymmetric because its face diagonals are not distributed symmetrically. To reduce this, I split the diagonal on every cube face before subdivision, producing four triangles around each face center. The preprocessed cube subdivides into a noticeably more symmetric rounded shape.
